Species diversity increases information flow about predation in bird communities

PLOS Biology · 10.1371/journal.pbio.3003805

The Biodiversity-Ecosystem Functioning (BEF) literature has shown species diversity to be essential for ecosystem functioning and services. Yet this concept has not been extended often to behavioral ecology, so that although it is understood that acquiring social information from heterospecific individuals is an important component of ecosystem functioning, it is unclear how animal signaling at the community level, and subsequent information flow, is related to increased species diversity. Eliciting vocal responses using predator models across 10 nature reserves on a latitudinal gradient, we first show that the species diversity of birds is correlated with signaling about predation, both in the low-risk system of mobbing and in the higher-risk system of alarm calls. After finding a similar result across a fragment area gradient in one reserve, we used this system to test how interspecific information flow is affected by species diversity. Potential mechanisms behind this effect include that species are complementary in the characteristics of their mobbing calls, so more species translates into more cumulative information, or there are higher signaling rates as more individuals are involved, or a specific keystone species is more likely to occur. This experiment varied the number of species producing mobbing calls (one, two, four, eight species), with two playback construction methods (standardizing call rate, as typical for BEF experiments, or letting it increase with species richness, as in natural assemblages). Heterospecific mobbing participation (number of vocal species, number of participating individuals), and responses (call rate, probability to call in the first minute and frequency range) increased with species richness. Construction method significantly interacted with species richness, as non-standardized exemplars showed accentuated response as species richness increased. These results match expectations that complementary differences among species in the acoustic characteristics of mobbing calls, and increased call rate, both contribute to the result. There was little evidence that the signals of one particular species changed the experimental result. The overall evidence thus supports the hypothesis that the species diversity of a community is a key component influencing the quantity and the characteristics of signaling and information flow inside it.
